Iceland in October: what the weather actually does

Twelve minutes of reading that will save you a month of forum threads.

Halla SigurdardottirFirstlight guide, Iceland · Updated this season
DisclosureFirstlight sells a nine-day October trip in Iceland, so we are not a neutral party. Everything below is what I tell friends who are going independently. Where a paid link appears, it is labelled.

October in Iceland is not a compromise month. It is a different country from July, and for a lot of travelers it is the better one. The buses have thinned out, the guesthouses that fill six months ahead in summer have rooms in the same week you call, and the light does something in October that it simply does not do at midsummer, when the sun is up for twenty-one hours and everything is flat and bright.

What you give up is certainty. Some days in October are still and clear and warm enough to stand outside without gloves. Some days are horizontal rain at forty miles an hour and every viewpoint on the south coast is a grey wall. You do not get to choose. What you can do is understand the odds, plan for the bad version, and be genuinely delighted by the good one.

Daylight, week by week

This is the number that surprises people most, and it changes fast. Iceland loses roughly seven minutes of daylight per day through October.

Approximate daylight hours in Reykjavik by week in October

1 to 7 Octoberaround 07:40around 18:40roughly 11 hours
8 to 14 Octoberaround 08:05around 18:10roughly 10 hours
15 to 21 Octoberaround 08:30around 17:40roughly 9 hours
22 to 31 Octoberaround 09:00around 17:10roughly 8 hours

Times are for Reykjavik and shift by fifteen to twenty minutes in the north and east. Note the last row carefully. In the final week of October you have about eight hours of light, and Iceland's clocks do not change, so there is no extra hour on offer. If your plan involves five stops separated by two hours of driving, it does not fit in the day. Cut it to three.

The compensation is that the sun never climbs high. Sunrise light lasts about ninety minutes rather than twenty. Golden hour in October is closer to golden morning and golden afternoon, which is why photographers who know the island come now rather than in June.

Temperature, and why it is the wrong thing to worry about

Reykjavik averages roughly 38°F to 45°F in October, and the north runs three to five degrees colder. Those numbers are mild. Americans arriving from Minnesota or Colorado tend to look at them and pack for a cool autumn. That is the mistake.

The variable that matters is wind. Iceland regularly delivers 30 to 50 mph sustained wind on exposed coastline, and at 40°F with driving rain and that kind of wind, you will be genuinely cold in ten minutes if your outer layer is not properly waterproof and windproof. A warm coat that soaks through is worse than useless.

Pack for wind and water, not for cold. If your jacket has a zip that is not covered and a hood that does not cinch, it will not do the job here.

The layer system that actually works

  • A wool or synthetic base layer, top and bottom. Not cotton, which stays wet.
  • A fleece or light down mid layer you can remove without taking the shell off.
  • A genuinely waterproof hard shell with a hood that tightens. Rated 10,000mm or better.
  • Waterproof trousers. Everyone skips these and everyone regrets it on the black beaches.
  • Boots with ankle support and real tread. Loose volcanic gravel is unforgiving.
  • Gloves, hat, buff. Not optional in the last two weeks of the month.

We send this list, with specific models, to everyone booked on our October departures. It is also the list I would use myself. Some gear links in our full packing guide are affiliate links, labelled where they appear; they do not change your price and I have used everything on it.

Roads: what closes, and when

The Ring Road, Route 1, stays open through October in all but exceptional weather. That is the good news, and it is the reason an October Ring Road trip is realistic while a January one is not.

What closes is the interior. The F-roads across the highlands, including the routes to Landmannalaugar and Askja, shut progressively from mid-September through October depending on snow. By the end of the month, assume they are gone. If an itinerary you are reading includes the highlands in late October, it was written for summer and nobody updated it.

Two practical habits, both free:

  1. Check road.is every morning before you drive. It shows live closures and surface conditions in English, and it is accurate.
  2. Check vedur.is for the weather and, more importantly, the wind warnings. An orange wind warning means do not open your car door into the wind. That is not a figure of speech; it is how most rental car damage happens here.

The northern lights question

October is inside aurora season. Nights are long enough, the sky is dark enough, and displays do happen. I have watched good ones from a guesthouse car park near Myvatn in the second week of October more than once.

Now the honest part. Aurora requires two independent things to line up: solar activity, which nobody controls, and clear sky, which in Iceland in October is roughly a coin flip on any given night. Multiply those together and a single night has modest odds. A week of nights outside the cities has decent odds. A guarantee is not a thing that exists, and any operator selling you one is selling you weather.

If aurora is the main reason for your trip, give yourself at least five nights, stay outside Reykjavik for most of them, and treat it as a bonus rather than the point. If it is the point and it does not happen, you will have spent a week disappointed in a country that had a great deal else to offer.

What things cost in October

October sits in the shoulder, and prices reflect it. Guesthouses outside Reykjavik commonly run 25 to 40 percent below their July rate. Rental cars drop further, sometimes by half. Flights from the U.S. east coast are usually at their lowest between the end of the summer schedule and the Christmas rush.

What does not drop is food. Iceland is expensive to eat in, in any month. A sit-down dinner outside the capital will typically run 4,500 to 7,500 ISK per person, and the small-town options are limited enough that booking ahead matters even in shoulder season. The supermarket chains Bonus and Kronan are how residents manage this, and there is no reason a visitor should not do the same for lunches.

A realistic October week

If I had seven days and a rental car, this is the shape I would give it, and it is deliberately less ambitious than most published itineraries:

  • Two nights on the south coast, based near Vik rather than driving back to the capital each day.
  • Two nights around Skaftafell and the glacier lagoon, which is the single stretch most worth the extra night.
  • One night in the eastern fjords, the part almost everyone skips and almost everyone who goes remembers.
  • Two nights north around Myvatn and Akureyri, then the domestic flight back rather than a long drive.

That leaves whole afternoons unscheduled. In October that is not laziness, it is the plan. The afternoon you keep free is the one you spend somewhere unexpected because the weather turned good, or the one you spend in a swimming pool while it rains sideways.

Should you go with an operator?

Honestly, plenty of people should not. Iceland is one of the easiest countries in the world to drive independently: the roads are good, the signage is in Latin script, almost everyone speaks fluent English, and the whole route is a single loop. If you are comfortable driving in wind and rain and you enjoy planning, do it yourself and keep the money.

Go with an operator if you would rather not drive at all in those conditions, if you want a resident guide explaining what you are looking at, if you are travelling alone and would like company for dinner, or if the idea of rebooking a guesthouse at 9pm because a road closed is your idea of a ruined holiday. That is the actual value, and it is worth paying for or it is not, depending on who you are.
